## What was the job?

A sports media startup needed a front end built from scratch for two properties.
DNVR was the established one. PHNX was the first build outside the home market,
in Phoenix, carrying a large blog and ecommerce memberships.

I designed and built the full WordPress front end for both. Not a theme with the
colors changed. Built.

The infrastructure belonged to a separate AWS developer, and I coordinated with
them across EC2, Elastic Beanstalk, RDS, S3 and Route 53.

## And then?

48 hours before launch, the site came off AWS. Mixed content issues and an
incomplete setup. The developer who owned that infrastructure went dark.

I want to be precise about my position here, because it is the part that makes
the story worth telling. I was the front-end guy. I had coordinated with the AWS
side, I had not built it, and I was not on call for it. None of that mattered by
about hour three, because the launch date was not moving and nobody else was
going to pick it up.

## What did I do?

I learned enough AWS, fast, to understand a stack I had only ever worked
alongside. Not to fix it myself, to be able to describe exactly what was broken
and why, in words another engineer could act on.

Then I went to the CEO directly and told them what I had found, what I thought
it would take, and what I thought their options were.

We brought in a replacement developer. I got them oriented on the system and out
of the discovery phase fast, and it launched with hours to spare.

One thing I want to keep straight, because it gets flattened every time this
story is retold: **I was not the emergency hire.** I was the person who found
one and got them up to speed.

## Decisions

**Diagnose first, recruit second.** Bringing in a developer with 48 hours on the
clock only works if they can start on the actual problem instead of spending
their first day discovering the system. Learning enough AWS to hand over a real
description of the failure is the entire reason the handoff took hours instead
of days. That is not heroics, it is just doing the unglamorous part first.

**Go to the CEO, not around them.** With the infrastructure owner unreachable
and a fixed date, the only decision that mattered was move the launch or replace
the developer. That was not my call to make. It was my job to get it in front of
the person whose call it was, early enough that they still had room to make it.

## What happened after

The relationship kept going. I improved AWS security that had been left weak,
fixed front-end bugs across both properties, and worked on UX.

## An honest note on the stack

I coordinated on the AWS side and worked in it directly during the incident and
afterward. I am listing those services because I genuinely worked in them under
pressure, not because I architected them. I did not.

The WordPress front end for both properties is mine, start to finish.
