## Recovering a bad launch

A large multi-location dental group came out of a site refresh that had gone
badly. I owned the CMS architecture and a full site redesign, working directly
with their in-house engineering team rather than around them.

Both the client and their own developer credited the work.

The layouts I built for location pages, designed for conversion and made
reusable rather than bespoke per client, were later adopted companywide.

## Decisions

**Work with the in-house engineering team instead of replacing them.** An agency
arriving after a failed launch has an easy story available: the previous work was
bad, hand it over. It is also the story that guarantees friction with the people
who will maintain the site afterward and know things about it that are not written
down. Treating their developer as a collaborator is the reason the recovery
produced a credit from both sides rather than a handoff.

**Build the location pages as a reusable layout, not a one-off.** Multi-location
businesses have the same page repeated with different content, which means the
work is either done once well or many times inconsistently. Designing the layout
to be reused is what let it travel to other accounts.

## Becoming the security lead

After a hack that exposed credentials, I became the team's cybersecurity subject
matter expert. I built and executed the remediation strategy that secured every
client account within 24 hours and closed the weak points that had been exploited.

**Why 24 hours and not a phased rollout.** With credentials exposed across a
shared book of accounts, the exposure window is the whole risk. A phased
remediation leaves a known-compromised credential live on some accounts while
others are being fixed, and an attacker with a list does not work through it in
the order you scheduled. Securing everything inside a day cost more coordination
than a staged plan and removed the window entirely.
